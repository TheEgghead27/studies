\section{Supply and Demand I}
\begin{outline}
    \term{Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)}
        curve representing maximum productivity (the downwards slope indicates a \textit{trade-off} between multiple outputs). Points beneath the curve can increase production without trade-offs (inefficiencies, underemployment), points above the curve are not feasible (yet).
        \2 The first-derivative of the PPF (``slope of tangent line'') represents the \textbf{opportunity cost} per additional item on the X-axis. In the lecture example, we had guns vs roses.
        \2 The ideal point to be on the PPF depends on consumer preferences.
        \2 Considering a PPF between consumption goods (for immediate use) and capital goods (for future use), the choice of point on the PPF influences future PPFs (more capital $\implies$ more production possibility).

    \term{Consumption Possibility Frontier (CPF)}
        demand-side analog to the PPF, CPF = PPF assuming no external trade.
    \term{Law of Demand}
        there exists an inverse (negative) relationship between price and quantity demanded.
        \2 The point at which the demand curve intersects the price-axis\footnote{Due to the convention set by Alfred Marshall, price is usually on the y-axis despite the fact we more often use price as the independent variable.} ($Q_D=0$) is the \textbf{choke price of demand}.
        \2 We approximate supply curves as ramp functions going up along the price-axis until the choke price.
    \term{Law of Demand}
        there exists a direct (positive) relationship between price and quantity supplied.
        \2 The point at which the supply curve intersects the price-axis ($Q_S=0$) is the \textbf{choke price of supply} as determined by production costs.
        \2 We approximate demand curves as reverse ramp functions going down along the price-axis until the choke price.
    \term{Equilibrium} The \textbf{equilibrium price} and \textbf{equilibrium quantity} are the stable-state\footnote{In intermediate microeconomics, the notion of unstable equilibria is introduced.} values $(P^*, Q*)$ at the intersection of supply and demand curves.
        \2 Given $P_{High} > P^*$, $Q_S>Q_D$, a \textbf{surplus}.
        \2 Given $P_{Low} < P^*$, $Q_D>Q_S$, a \textbf{shortage}.
        \3 In both of these cases, the \textbf{price mechanism} will naturally cause the price to tend towards $P^*$, e.g. if the original supplier offers concert tickets at $P_L$, a secondary market will emerge.
        \2 When the equilibrium point lies on the price-axis (e.g. choke price of supply $>$ choke price of demand), the product is not produced. (Example: consumer jetpacks, flying cars, nuclear missile-launching watch)
        \2 When the equilibrium point for a good lies on the supply-axis (e.g. $P^*=0)$, that good is considered a \textbf{free good} like air.
\end{outline}
In short, the \textbf{market} decides \textit{what} is produced and how \textit{much} to produce. This can change based on additional factors like technology and taste (see equations \ref{eq:1.1} and \ref{eq:1.2}).

\begin{outline}
    \term{Price Ceiling}
    a (government-)imposed maximum price on a good.
    \2 If the ceiling is above the market price, it is a non-binding constraint.
    \2 If the ceiling is below the market price, a shortage will result due to decreased supply and increased demand. Some people may benefit from the artificially lowered price, but there will also emerge a black market where a higher equilibrium price emerges, changing the quantity demanded.
    \term{Price Floor}
    a (government-)imposed minimum price on a good.
    \2 Taking the labor market for example, a higher minimum wage will lead to layoffs of low-value workers (for a firm will only hire employees worth more to them than the salary demanded). There will be a surplus of labor (unemployment). Removing the minimum wage allows for movement down the supply curve (less job-seekers, more people occupied in education or whatnot) and up the demand curve (hiring more people).
    \termb{Consumer Surplus}
    $= \int_0^{Q^*} \left(D(Q) - P^*\right) \,dQ$, the area under the demand curve and above the equilibrium price. This represents how much more some consumers \textit{would} have paid, but do not have to in this market.
    \termb{Producer Surplus}
    $= \int_0^{Q^*} \left(P^*-S(Q)\right) \,dQ$, the area above the demand curve and below the equilibrium price. This represents how much less some producers \textit{would} have sold their product at, but do not have to in this market.
    \2 An auction, or any other manner of (perfect) price discrimination minimizes consumer surplus.
    \termb{Allocative Efficiency} $= \textrm{Consumer Surplus} + \textrm{Producer Surplus}$
    \term{Gross Price $P^g$} The money a consumer pays for a good including taxes.
    \term{Net Price $P^n$} The money a producer receives for a good after taxes.
\end{outline}

\subsection{Taxation}
When a tax is levied on a good, the resultant $Q_t<Q^*$ (either from a shift in supply from taxing the producer, or a shift in demand from taxing the consumer). Taxation on an economic activity \textit{decreases} economic activity. There is less consumer surplus and less producer surplus due to the introduction of a \textbf{government surplus (tax revenue)} $=\int_0^{Q_t}\left(P^{g}(Q)-P^{n}(Q)\right)\,dt$ and a \textbf{dead weight loss} (the gap between the original S/D curves over $Q_t$ and $Q^*$).
\begin{outline}
    \termb{Total (Societal) Surplus} $=\textrm{Consumer Surplus} + \textrm{Government Surplus} + \textrm{Producer Surplus}$
    \term{Tax Incidence} The set of entities that a tax burden (mostly) falls on.
\end{outline}
In general, tax burden is \textit{shared} among consumer and producer. The collection of the tax is logistically cheaper when taken from the producer.

\section{Elasticity}
\subsection{Own Price Elasticity of Demand}
The (own) price elasticity of demand (at a specific point $(P,Q)$) is the
$$\epsilon = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P} = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}}{\frac{\Delta P}{P}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{Q}\frac{P}{\Delta Q} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \frac{P}{Q}$$
The final form shows that the inverse slope on a Q/P graph can be used to evaluate elasticity. Note that some textbooks report only the absolute value of $\epsilon<0$, we include the negative sign in this course.

From geometry, an alternative formula for the elasticity at point $(P,Q)$ on a linear demand curve with Q-intercept $I$ is \[\epsilon = \frac{I-Q}{Q}\]

The \textbf{Midpoint Formula} form of elasticity is
\[\epsilon = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{\frac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}}}{\frac{\Delta P}{\frac{P_1+P_2}{2}}}\]
For a linear demand curve, elasticity decreases as the quantity sold increases (we move down the demand curve).

Demand for a good and its relationship to \textbf{total revenue} ($TR=P\cdot Q$) is summarized in table \ref{tab:3.1}.
Plotting the total revenue on the Q-axis of a linear demand curve yields a parabola\footnote{The total revenue parabola is symmetric, but the total cost of production will monotonically increase. Thus, to maximize profit $\Pi = TR-TC$, firms remain on the left side of the curve, or most optimally, the midpoint.} with maximal revenue at $\epsilon=1$. For prices left of (lower) than the vertex, there is elastic demand: a price cut (hike) increases (decreases) revenue, and to the right the inverse is true.

The price elasticity of demand depends on factors such as the
\begin{outline}
	\1 Availability of close substitutes.
	\1 Time to adjust to substitutes -- as consumers have more time to churn to cheaper options, they will do so.
	\1 Proportion of consumer budget allocated to the item -- e.g. shoelaces are a minor and trivial expense
\end{outline}

\subsection{Income Elasticity}
The income elasticity considers the change in demand relative to income.
\[
    \epsilon_I = \frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta INC}
\]
We classify goods based on the value of $\epsilon_I$ as
\begin{outline}
    \term{Normal Goods} $0<\epsilon_I$.

        \2 $0<\epsilon_I<1$ is a \textbf{necessity} good.
        \2 $1<\epsilon$ is a \textbf{luxury} good.
    \termb{Inferior Goods}\footnote{Note that inferior goods are still goods, not ``bads'', they do not bring harm.} --
    $\epsilon_I<0$.
        \2 In an $N$-good world, $N-1$ goods can be inferior, as the last good must be able to soak up additional demand (see section \ref{sec:consumer}).
    \end{outline}

\subsection{Cross Price Elasticity}
The cross price elasticity compares the change in quantity demanded for $Y$ relative to the price of another good $X$.

\[
    CPE = \frac{\%\Delta Q_D^Y}{\%\Delta P^x}
\]
We classify the relation between goods as
\begin{outline}
	\term{Compliments} $CPE<0$
	\term{Substitutes} $CPE>0$
\end{outline}

\subsection{Applications}
\begin{outline}
	\1 The existence of substitutes is often raised as coutner-evidence to accusations of monopoly.
	\1 The provision of food aid, while artificially moving the supply curve rightward (increasing), decreases total revenue for domestic firms, disincentivising production.
	    \2 The terms of trade for agricultural producs are also unfavorable for developing countries, as the elasticity of demand for food products means investing in increasing production may not meaningfully increase revenues.
	\1 Tax disincentives:
	    \2 A tax on something with inelastic demand like cigarettes (i.e. a \textbf{sin tax}) is not going to effectively reduce the sin, but will raise substantial tax revenue (mostly consumer burden) without humongous dead-weight loss.
	    \2 A tax on something with elastic demand like blue shirts will severely pervert incentives, resulting in a massive dead weight loss and producer burden (but the tax burden is still \textit{shared} with consumers).
	\1 Perfect Cases:
	    \2 A \textbf{perfectly inelastic} demand curve is a vertical line (no matter what price, that amount of product will be bought).
		\2 A \textbf{perfectly elastic} demand curve is a horizontal line (even the slightest increase in price will cause demand to go to $0$).
\end{outline}

\section{Consumer Behavior/Preferences}\label{sec:consumer}
The fundamental neoclassical theory of consumer behavior/preferences is that
\begin{outline}
	\1 Companies maximize profit.
	\1 Consumers maximize happiness.
	    \2 We assume that there are cardinal (countable) units of happiness called \textbf{utils}.
	    \2 The utility function takes the form of $u=f(x_1,x_2,...)$, where each $x_i$ is the quantity of a good or service to buy.
\end{outline}
To characterize $u$, we make some assumptions about consumer preferences, namely that
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Completeness -- A consumer can always make a choice
	\item Transitivity -- If a consumer prefers apples to bananas, and bananas to carrots, they must also prefer apples to carrots.\footnote{Notice that whether these are substitutes or compliments depends on the situation, e.g. the fruits do not compete in a fruit salad.}
	\item Nonsatiation -- There are ``unlimited wants,'' more is always better.
\end{enumerate}
Graphing the \textbf{total utility} ($TU$) for a given product, we find that the function is \textit{monotonically increasing} (as per the assumption of nonsatiation), but the \textbf{marginal utility} ($MU$, per-item derivative) is decreasing (but positive). This is referred to as the \textbf{Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility}, which makes it rational to purchase a diverse spread of goods instead of repeatedly buying one thing.

We maximize $u$ subject to the \textbf{income constraint} that
\[\textrm{Income} = \sum_{i=1}^N x_i P_i\]
which excludes the possibility of saving ($\ge$) or borrowing ($\le$). Under such conditions, the consumer optimum would be achieved when
\[\frac{MU_x}{P_x}=\frac{MU_y}{P_y}\] i.e. the utility per dollar has been maximized (given a non-optimum state, the rational course of action would be to reduce $P_i$ for the $i$ with lowest marginal utility per dollar, and use the returned money to buy something with more marginal utility per dollar).

\subsection{The Income and Substitution Effects}
The \textbf{income effect} represents how much relative purchasing power the consumer will allocate to this good. The \textbf{substitution effect} represents the consumer choosing a cheaper subtitute.

For most cases, this will result in a downward sloping curve. We consider a price change $\Delta P > 0$.
\begin{outline}
	\1 For a normal good, the consumer has less relative purchasing power AND they would rather buy something cheaper, so $\Delta Q < 0$ and the demand curve is downward sloping as expected.
	\1 For an inferior good, the income effect actually suggests an increase in demand, but the substitution effect usually wins out.
	    \2 In the rare case that the substitution effect does not win out, the good is called a \textbf{Giffen Good} experiencing the \textbf{Giffen Paradox}. All Giffen goods are inferior.
\end{outline}
